## The claim

One trade per session on a single high-volatility stock: wait for the opening range, enter on the breakout, stop and target fixed in percent. The backtest showed **+557% over 98 sessions** at 3× leverage, with an annualised Sharpe of 7.55. Numbers like that are usually wrong somewhere. The job was to find out where.

## 1 — Rebuild it independently

I rebuilt the strategy from the written rules on independently sourced 1-minute data. My trades matched the original's direction on **97% of sessions**, so the logic was real and reproducible. The Sharpe of 7.55 was also easy to decompose: it is a per-trade Sharpe of 0.49 multiplied by √252. That's correct arithmetic, but it assumes a trade every day at that quality.

## 2 — Price the execution honestly

The original backtest filled at the most favourable price inside the trigger bar and assumed zero slippage. Re-filling at prices you could actually get removed about **30% of the reported P&L**. The edge survived, but smaller.

## 3 — Ask what it is actually long

High-volatility days paid about twice as much as calm ones. The strategy was a **long-volatility** bet in disguise, not a trend or bull-market artefact. So I ran the identical rules on the same stock in its calm prior year.

## 4 — Fix it, and test the fix out of sample

Two changes, both picked on a training window and judged walk-forward:

- **Volatility-scaled trigger.** The breakout threshold scales with recent volatility, so noise on quiet mornings stops triggering entries.
- **Volatility gate.** Trade only when the prior 10-day realised volatility is above about 5%. The threshold was robust anywhere from 3% to 5%.

| Change | Effect (out of sample) |
| --- | --- |
| Vol-scaled trigger | Sharpe **+78%**, max drawdown **−35%**, whipsaw stop-outs 6 → 1, profit −9% |
| + vol gate, 2025 + 2026 combined | profit roughly flat (~$363k), Sharpe **0.22 → 0.50** |

## What didn't work

**Inverse-volatility position sizing** made things worse. It shrinks exposure exactly when the strategy earns its money, fighting the long-vol edge. I kept it in the report, because a negative result is part of understanding the strategy.

> A backtest is a claim about the future. The audit's job is to work out which part of the number is edge, which is execution, and which is regime.
